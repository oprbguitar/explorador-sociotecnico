## Region as historical structure in entity["country","Peru","south america"]

For entity["people","José Carlos Mariátegui","peruvian marxist thinker"], the “Indian problem” was not fundamentally moral, pedagogical, or racial; it was rooted in land, agrarian structure, and the afterlife of colonial domination. In the same work, he argued that regionalism and centralism in Peru could not be solved as a merely administrative problem, because they were anchored in deeper social and economic relations. That is the basic reason his thought still matters for Axis 1: Costa, Sierra, and Selva are not just landscapes but historically differentiated social formations. citeturn19search0turn19search1turn19search3turn19search9

entity["people","John V. Murra","andean ethnohistorian"] adds a different but complementary layer. Later discussions of the model continue to treat it as one of the most influential frameworks in Andean anthropology and ethnohistory. Read sociologically, Murra’s argument does more than explain production: it shows that territory, kinship, circulation, and economy were historically co-constructed in the Andes through ecological complementarity rather than through a flat, homogeneous space. citeturn18search12turn18search15turn18search3

Contemporary social indicators show that this territorial differentiation remains materially consequential. ## Coloniality, world-system pressures, and hybridization

entity["people","Aníbal Quijano","peruvian sociologist"]’s central intervention is that colonial domination did not end with political independence. In his account, modernity and coloniality are historically joined: the global order that emerged with the conquest of the Americas organized labor, race, authority, and knowledge in a single matrix that persisted after formal colonial rule. Duke’s edition of his foundational essays presents this as a distinct way of reading the contemporary world-system, and Quijano’s 1992 text explicitly links the formation of a global order to the conquest of Latin America and the persistence of direct political, social, and cultural domination beyond classical colonialism. citeturn44search0turn44search3turn35search0turn44search8

That argument connects directly to entity["people","Immanuel Wallerstein","world systems scholar"], whose modern world-system framework treats peripheral regions as integrated into capitalism through structured inequalities rather than through equal exchange. For Peru, the value of pairing Quijano with Wallerstein is analytic: Costa, Sierra, and Selva do not encounter “the global” in the same way, because their incorporation into national and transnational markets is filtered through unequal infrastructures, histories of state penetration, and racialized hierarchies. Recent World Bank documents still describe those disparities as structural, while sectoral research on Peruvian agriculture notes that the coast concentrates the most productive and export-oriented farming, whereas much of the Sierra and Selva remain more strongly characterized by small producers. citeturn35search2turn22search1turn41search12turn8search6

But Axis 2 cannot be reduced to domination alone. entity["people","Fernando Ortiz","cuban anthropologist"] introduced “transculturation” precisely to name a process more complex than one-way acculturation, and later scholarship still treats that concept as the key theoretical innovation of *Contrapunteo cubano del tabaco y el azúcar*. entity["people","Néstor García Canclini","cultural theorist"]’s notion of hybrid cultures develops a related proposition: external forms are selectively appropriated, reworked, and recombined in local settings rather than simply copied. This matters for Peru because it explains why global modernity can generate fast-food franchises, export agribusiness, migrant religious networks, and digital consumer culture without eliminating chifa, quinoa revival, popular markets, communal economies, or Indigenous territorial politics. Hybridization is real; Quijano’s warning is that hybridity unfolds inside an unequal field, not outside it. citeturn35search11turn35search3turn36search2turn36search13

entity["people","José Matos Mar","peruvian anthropologist"] turned that insight into a specifically Peruvian diagnosis. His argument in *Desborde popular y crisis del Estado* was that mass migration and popular urbanization were overflowing the institutional limits of the old state through parallel strategies of work, residence, circulation, and organization. That reading still feels empirically relevant. Official labor data for April 2024 to March 2025 reported that 70.7% of employed Peruvians were in informal employment. In 2024 the highest city-level informal employment rates appeared in Juliaca, Pucallpa, and Ayacucho, while Lima and Callao were lower but still reported 56.9%. Migration also has embodied and material consequences that fit the brief’s concern with action, globalization, and food. The PERU MIGRANT study and its follow-up analyses found that urban dwellers and rural-to-urban migrants had much higher obesity incidence than rural residents, and migrants showed a higher incidence of central obesity as well. That does not prove a simple causal chain from migration to identity change, but it does show that insertion into urban space, labor regimes, and commodity environments reorganizes daily practice at the level of the body itself. In that sense, Matos Mar’s “overflow” is not just political and spatial; it is metabolic and social. citeturn10search0turn10search8turn10search9

## Food transition and uneven nutritional change

entity["people","Barry M. Popkin","nutrition scholar"]’s nutrition-transition framework remains the clearest way to describe the shift from traditional diets toward more urban, industrial, and energy-dense food environments, while entity["people","Carlos A. Monteiro","brazilian nutrition scholar"]’s work on ultra-processed products identifies transnational food manufacturing, retail, and fast-food chains as central drivers of the contemporary global food system. The food-studies tradition associated with Sidney Mintz, Jack Goody, and Claude Fischler adds the cultural point that food is never only caloric intake; it is class, memory, prestige, discipline, and belonging. The result is a combined lens in which diets change through political economy and through meaning at once. citeturn14search1turn14search8turn14search2turn15search12turn14search10

For Peru, the epidemiological evidence strongly supports the existence of an uneven nutrition transition. A 2022 *Lancet Regional Health – Americas* review stated that Peru has experienced nutritional transition for at least three decades, and a 2025 modeling study argued that the country now faces a double burden in which progress against stunting has stalled since 2018 while overweight continues to rise. Official ENDES 2024 results show that anemia among children aged 6 to 35 months remained markedly higher in the Sierra (43.1%) and Selva (44.1%) than on the Costa (27.0%). This is the opposite of a simple “traditional bad / modern good” story: industrialization and urbanization do not dissolve undernutrition, and they coexist with rising overweight and diet-related chronic risks. citeturn7search10turn7search3turn6search18

The ultra-processed-food evidence is also substantial. A 2025 Peruvian study estimated that among children aged 6 to 35 months, ultra-processed foods contributed 27% of total energy intake and were consumed by 86% of participants. Another study in Peruvian adults found that ultra-processed-food intake was associated with waist circumference, with the association especially evident in rural settings. Those findings matter for Axis 4 because they show that globalized food systems are not only urban or upper-class phenomena; they penetrate early childhood and rural life as well. citeturn7search0turn7search14turn7search12

At the same time, region still matters. Research with Shawi communities in the Peruvian Amazon describes Indigenous perceptions of food-system change as bound up with policy, climate stress, and pragmatic decision-making, while work with Awajún communities found that traditional food diversity predicts better dietary quality. In parallel, Peru’s response to processed and ultra-processed foods has included front-of-package octagonal warnings under Law 30021 and its later regulatory instruments, explicitly targeting products high in sugar, sodium, saturated fat, or trans fat. So the empirical picture is not one of simple replacement. It is one of overlap: traditional systems persist, weaken, are commodified, or are strategically defended under pressure from industrial supply chains and regulation. citeturn8search5turn8search1turn8search13turn43search13turn43search16turn43search18

## Identity, ethnicity, and the national frame

entity["people","Stuart Hall","cultural theorist"]’s formulation is essential here: identities are not fixed essences but historically constructed positions, constantly made and remade across practices, discourses, and antagonisms. entity["people","Fredrik Barth","norwegian anthropologist"] shifts the focus from “cultural content” to boundaries maintained and renegotiated in interaction, and entity["people","Benedict Anderson","nationalism scholar"] gives the classic definition of the nation as an imagined political community. Together, they imply that Peruvian identity cannot be adequately described as a stable national core with regional variations attached to it afterward. Rather, “Peru” is continuously assembled through language, schooling, migration, media, food, racial hierarchy, and symbolic boundaries. citeturn12search0turn37search2turn37search6turn37search3turn37search15

Peruvian scholarship makes that proposition concrete. entity["people","Marisol de la Cadena","peru anthropologist"] shows that “mestizo” in Cusco is not a neutral demographic label but a negotiated political-cultural identity that can claim Indigenous heritage without abolishing the deep inequalities of racism. entity["people","José María Arguedas","peruvian writer anthropologist"], from a literary-anthropological angle, dramatizes Peru as a lived tension between Andean and criollo-Western worlds, not as an already reconciled nation. Official cultural data reinforce the point: Peru recognizes 55 Indigenous peoples and 48 Indigenous languages, with the overwhelming majority of those languages located in the Amazon. Identity in Peru is therefore territorially thick from the start. citeturn13search0turn12search10turn28search2turn28search1turn28search9

The poverty profile makes the hierarchy visible even within contemporary self-description. In 2024, the poor population had a larger share of native-language background than the non-poor population (19.0% versus 15.0%). The same report shows that the poor population had a larger share of self-identified native origin than the non-poor population (32.1% versus 27.4%), while the non-poor population had a larger mestizo share than the poor population (52.1% versus 42.0%). These figures do not mean that language or ethnicity mechanically determine poverty. ## Hypothesis testing and unresolved tensions

**H1 is broadly supported, but only partially resolved at the regional scale.** The theory predicts that stronger insertion into global market circuits should increase the presence of industrial and ultra-processed food in local diets. Peruvian evidence supports that direction: the country has undergone decades of nutrition transition, UPFs already account for a large share of energy among very young children, and adult studies connect UPF intake with adiposity-related indicators. But the regionally differentiated picture remains incomplete. Official data clearly show distinct nutritional burdens across Costa, Sierra, and Selva, yet the Peru-specific UPF studies retrieved here do not provide a single unified comparison across the three macro-regions by class, age, gender, and ethnicity all at once. citeturn7search10turn7search0turn7search12turn6search18

**H2 is partially supported.** Matos Mar’s framework and later migration-health evidence strongly support the claim that Andean-to-urban migration reorganizes life chances, livelihoods, and embodied practice. The cohort evidence on obesity among rural-to-urban migrants is especially persuasive on material transformation. The cultural part of the hypothesis—hybrid identity through residence, work, consumption, and language—is theoretically well aligned with García Canclini, but in the sources retrieved here it is better established conceptually than directly measured for contemporary Andean migrants in coastal cities. The strongest empirical evidence available is about urban restructuring and changing practices, not about a standardized identity index. citeturn20search0turn20search2turn10search0turn10search9turn36search2

**H3 is the most robust conceptually and reasonably strong empirically.** Quijano provides the mechanism of symbolic subordination through race, labor, and knowledge hierarchies; de la Cadena shows how those hierarchies survive inside categories such as mestizo; and official poverty profiles show that native-language and native-origin populations are still overrepresented among the poor relative to the non-poor. ## Evidence gaps

The evidence gaps in the brief are real and not just rhetorical. Official Peruvian surveys now provide strong breakdowns by natural region, poverty condition, language, and ethnic self-perception, and recent health studies add specific findings on UPFs, anemia, obesity, and Indigenous food systems. But these streams rarely converge in one integrated research design. The result is that Peru has good evidence for territorial inequality, good evidence for dietary transition, and meaningful evidence for Indigenous territoriality, yet much weaker direct evidence linking all three to identity transformation in the same people across Costa, Sierra, and Selva over time. citeturn25view0turn27view0turn38view1turn39view0turn7search0turn8search5turn8search13

The thinnest area in the retrieved literature is the one you flagged around the contemporary “self” under digital mediation. The sources located here say a great deal about food systems, migration, poverty, language, and Indigenous territoriality, but far less about how regionally differentiated Peruvians are remaking subjectivity under social media, digital advertising, and platform consumption. The next serious research step would need to combine official household and health surveys with ethnography in migrant neighborhoods and Indigenous communities, plus media-use and consumption data. Without that, the causal chain from globalization to diet to identity remains theoretically plausible and partially evidenced, but not fully demonstrated in a single contemporary Peruvian comparison.
